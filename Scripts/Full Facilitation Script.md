### Slide 11

That brings us to what it means to be neurodiversity affirming. The starting point is that differences are not deficits. There are different ways of knowing, doing, being and belonging, and everyone has strengths and experiences challenges.

### Slide 12

The social model helps us put that curiosity to work. On this slide, we're using an environment-first lens. We look for barriers in the place, the task, the routine and the expectations. Then we ask what can change around the student.

Here's an imaginary classroom example. A class is gathering on the floor for a story. The usual expectation is that everyone sits close together, in the same space, for the whole reading. One student finds that arrangement difficult.

Before deciding what the student needs to do differently, we can look at what this routine asks of them. How crowded is the space? What choices are there about where to sit? Is sitting in that particular place necessary to take part in the story?

We might explore another place to sit, or a different way of joining. Those are possibilities to investigate with the student. We haven't established what will help just by coming up with an option.

The next two steps on the slide matter: include the student's communication and experience, and review whether the change makes participation more workable. What can the student tell or show us about the change? Is it helping them take part?

Changing an environment won't make every difficulty disappear. It gives us somewhere useful to begin, with barriers and supports that we can notice, discuss and review.

### Slide 16

Autism can be understood as difference and as a dynamic disability. Holding both ideas matters. Recognising difference and strengths does not mean overlooking difficulty or the need for substantial support.

Access and support needs can change with the task, the environment, relationships and the capacity available at that time. Something a student can do in one setting, or on one day, may not be available in another. We need to look at what is happening around the student as well as what the task is asking of them.

That takes us away from a fixed judgement about what someone can always do. It invites a more useful question: what is making participation possible here, and what support is needed now?

Keep the individual in view. There isn't one pattern that will explain every Autistic student's experience. Strengths, difficulties and fluctuating capacity all belong in the picture we build with the student and the people who know them.

### Slide 4

Many of us learnt five senses: touch, vision, taste, hearing and smell. This workshop uses eight. The other three are proprioception, vestibular sense and interoception.

Touch includes information about texture, pressure and temperature. Vision gives information about things such as colour, shape, depth and position. Gustatory and oral experience includes taste, texture, temperature and the sensation of chewing. Hearing includes pitch, loudness, distance and familiar sounds. Smell can also be closely connected with memory.

Proprioception gives us information about where our body is in space, drawing on signals from our muscles and joints. Pressure or movement may be something a student seeks for that input. The vestibular sense relates to balance and movement, including the speed and direction of head movement. Rocking or spinning may serve a regulating purpose for some students.

### Slide 9

This slide introduces four sensory response patterns from occupational therapist Winnie Dunn's framework. You may encounter this language in an occupational therapy report or a Sensory Profile School Companion questionnaire. The patterns are useful lenses, rather than fixed types of person.

Registration describes a pattern of noticing less sensory information or missing cues that others notice. Seeking describes actively looking for more input, perhaps through movement, touch or sound.

Sensitivity describes noticing sensory input readily and being affected by it, without necessarily taking action to reduce it. Avoiding describes actively trying to reduce input, perhaps by moving away from a crowd or covering the ears.

The slide also uses under-responsive and over-responsive language, and distinguishes more passive responses from active ways of changing the input. Keep that distinction connected to the student's experience, rather than treating the labels as a judgement.

A student may show different patterns for different senses. Their responses may also change with the context, the load and the day. We are looking at nervous-system responses, not simply a decision to cooperate or avoid a task.

So use the framework to sharpen your questions. What input is present? What has the student communicated? What pattern might be worth exploring? An observation gives us a starting point, not certainty about what is happening inside.

### Slide 2

Let’s begin with communication. Speech is one way of communicating, and we’ll be looking at a much wider picture: how students receive information, how they share what matters to them, and the ways we make both possible.
The amount someone speaks doesn’t tell us how much they understand. A student may need different ways to understand an instruction, answer a question, ask for help, request a pause or say no. All of those messages matter. Communication access belongs in lesson and task design in every subject, and across the whole school day.
We’ll talk about practical options, including augmentative and alternative communication, or A A C. There isn’t one approach that suits every student. Individual assessment and intervention questions belong with the student’s speech pathologist or relevant team; our focus here is what educators can notice, make available and change.

### Slide 3

Let’s come back together. We move between ways of communicating all day, often without noticing. Writing, facial expression, gesture, sign, a communication device and speech are all real communication. One method isn’t more valid because it is spoken.
That gives us something useful to notice at school. When we say, ‘Use your words,’ are we overlooking a message a student has already communicated? A point, a look, or moving closer or further away can be worth attending to. Our job is to listen across the ways that person communicates, while checking what a message means rather than assuming.

### Slide 4

There are two parts to keep distinct here: understanding and expressing. Understanding is information coming in and making sense. That might be a spoken instruction, a written sequence, a demonstration, or a gesture in context. Expressing is a message going out: an idea, a feeling, a question or a choice, through any communication method.
Across secondary school, that information includes instructions from several teachers, subject vocabulary, assessment details, timetable changes and what happens in corridors or at breaks.
These don’t necessarily look the same for one person. Someone may speak at length and still need support to understand spoken information. Someone else may understand much more than they can readily express. Neither fluent speech nor limited speech gives us the whole picture. Communication can also change with the person, the place, the demands and how someone is feeling.
A A C means augmentative and alternative communication. It can include gesture, signs, a communication board or book, typing, or a communication device. Some people use it alongside speech and some instead of speech.
Repeated or scripted words can carry meaning too. Listen for the context and possible purpose before correcting the form. We don’t need to assign a language-processing label before respecting a student’s communication. If we need an individual assessment, we involve the relevant team. For our classroom practice, the question is: what does this environment help the student understand, and what ways does it give them to respond?

### Slide 8

The first option is clear communication. Use familiar, specific words and give one idea or step at a time, in a form and amount the person can access. That might mean making a direction shorter or making the first step clearer.
The second is processing time. After giving information, pause. A person may be making sense of what you said, organising a response and communicating it. If we fill every pause with a new version of the question, we may add more information before the earlier information has been processed.
The third is visual support: showing information as well as saying it, so there is something to return to.
With several teachers in a day, useful guidance needs to be shared so each teacher knows what helps. These are options to match to the person and the moment, rather than a checklist that guarantees success. Keep the student’s A A C available and accept their preferred ways of responding, including refusal. A useful communication support makes messages easier to understand and share. It doesn’t require the student to produce the answer or communication style we were expecting.

### Slide 9

Visual supports are information you can see and come back to. The three examples here answer different questions. A timer can show how much time is left. A sequence can show what to do next. A daily schedule can show what is happening today. You may already use a calendar, a shopping list or a reminder on your phone in much the same way.
The format needs to make sense to the person. Objects, photographs, written words and symbols can all be options, on paper or on a device. Check that the student understands what the information represents and can reach it when it is needed. A support displayed across the room may exist without being useful at the moment of need.
For adolescents, formats such as a phone calendar, written task list or checklist in the learning platform may feel more comfortable because other students use them too. Ask about the format and whether it feels useful and respectful.
Think of the visual information already available in your classes: lesson steps, assessment instructions, the timetable and room changes. What question does it answer? Could a student return to it without asking an adult? A visual support offers information; it isn’t a condition the student must satisfy to access something they enjoy.
Visual supports and A A C can overlap, but they aren’t interchangeable. Keep the person’s communication methods available as well. We’ll return to this in Session Four when you choose a design option for a lesson, assessment task or handover.

### Slide 10

Double empathy asks us to look at understanding in both directions. When people have different experiences and ways of communicating, it can be harder for them to understand one another. That doesn’t mean every social misunderstanding sits inside the Autistic student.
Consider a class discussion, group task, corridor interaction or online conversation. Whose expectations are shaping what counts as joining in? Who is expected to adjust? What would make this interaction clearer or safer for the people involved?
For example, an adult might expect a quick spoken response as a sign of connection. A student might connect through a shared activity, through detailed talk about an interest, or with more time between turns. Those are possible differences to explore, not conclusions to impose on a particular student.
The question is how each student connects. Connection doesn’t need to look one way. Our expectations and the context shape the interaction too, so we have something useful to examine and change. We can make room for different ways of being together while continuing to listen to what each person actually wants.

### Slide 12

A student’s social world can include family, school, community and online spaces. Connection might be one to one, working alongside someone, gaming, sharing a great deal about an interest, or seeing someone only occasionally. Being together quietly can matter as much as a lively conversation.
Culture also shapes expectations about interaction and relationships. We need to be careful about treating the pattern most familiar to us as the measure of meaningful connection for everyone.
There may be spaces outside home and school where a student feels a particular sense of belonging: a club, a community activity, or another shared-interest space. For many adolescents, online connections and shared-interest groups are valued parts of their social lives too. We don’t need to disclose a student’s online activity, identity or community membership to recognise that these possibilities exist.
Keep the picture individual. What do we know about this student’s preferred people, places and ways of joining? What are we assuming? What could we find out respectfully?

### Slide 15

Think of a student’s available energy as a battery. Each day starts with some charge, and it isn’t necessarily the same amount as yesterday. Activities and demands draw on that energy. As the battery runs lower, managing the next demand can become harder.
You may also hear people in the Autistic community talk about spoons. The idea is having a limited supply available: an action uses one spoon or several, and when spoons are low, there is less left for the next thing. These are ways of talking about capacity, rather than a measure we can calculate for someone else.
The other part of the picture is recharging. The right supports can reduce demand and help restore some capacity. A pause, useful information, a preferred way to communicate or a safe connection may make a difference, depending on the person.
So we’ll look for both sides in a school day: what draws on capacity, and what helps to restore it. What helps one student may be different for another.

### Slide 16

Capacity fluctuates. Period six may look very different from period one. Like all of us, Autistic students can have easier days and harder days. Stress, energy, sensory demands and what else is happening in life can change what is manageable.
That means the same student may do something one day and need more support with it the next. Yesterday’s success doesn’t prove that today’s difficulty is a choice, and finishing a task doesn’t tell us how much effort it required.
Hold that in mind when you look at the students you know. We can ask what is different today, what demands have already accumulated, and what support is available now. We don’t have to know every cause before making information clearer or allowing a useful pause. We do need to keep checking with the student and the people who know them, instead of treating capacity as fixed.

### Slide 17

Support is a continuum, rather than one intervention that answers every need. Start with an inclusive environment: predictable routines across classes, clear expectations, assessment schedules published early, and a school culture where different ways of participating are accepted and valued.
Then consider universal supports available to many students. Instructions can be offered in spoken, written and visual forms. Students may have flexible ways to demonstrate learning, with exemplars and a class page where task information stays available.
Targeted support responds to a more specific need. For example, a small group might benefit from key vocabulary being introduced before a lesson, or from check-ins on assessment milestones.
Individual adjustments respond to the particular student’s access and participation needs. Examples might include a quieter workspace for an assessment, additional processing time, a personalised task breakdown or another way to demonstrate learning. What is appropriate depends on the student and the context, informed by consultation and evidence.
Look back at the design check. What is available to everyone? What might need to be targeted? What needs individual adjustment? Academic adjustments sit within this wider picture. These six principles come from the Family-School Partnerships Framework. Let's connect each one to what we do.
