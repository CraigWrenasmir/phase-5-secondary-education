### Slide 2

Belonging, capacity and joy bring the threads of today together. Keep all three in the picture when you consider whether a change is helping a student flourish.
Belonging means being recognised, included and able to participate as oneself. Capacity asks us to consider what the day costs, what it protects and what helps restore energy. Joy includes interests, connection and authentic expression.
These ideas connect our neurodiversity-affirming lens, our learning design across the secondary day and our partnerships. We begin with the person, learn about their experience, and bring student, family and school knowledge into the decisions.
Look again at the design change you chose in Session Four. It may reduce a barrier to participation. Could it also support belonging or joy? How might it affect what the day asks of the student? Remember the questions from Session Three: what costs, and what lightens?
Grades and attendance can be part of the picture, but it won't tell us everything about a student's experience. Quiet compliance or doing something without support is not, by itself, evidence of flourishing. Keep returning to the student, and to belonging, capacity and joy, as you judge what your change is making possible.

Let's bring the strongest ideas together at your table. Open page twenty-six of your Participant Guide.
Each person has about a minute to share the strongest thing they're taking back to school. Choose something that has stayed with you: an insight, a question or a practical change. You can draw on the gallery walk from Session Four as well as your own planning.
Then make a shared shortlist of three ideas from the table that are most worth spreading. For each idea, name who needs to hear it, and the meeting or moment where you could share it. Be as concrete as you can. Name the actual faculty meeting, year level meeting or staff briefing, and the colleague you need to speak with. A particular person and opportunity will help you see what to do next.

### Slide 4

Turn to page twenty-seven, Start, Continue, Reconsider. Place your Session Four commitment beside it, especially the line, this week in my own classes.
Under Start, choose a small strategy you can begin next week. It can be the same this-week change you have already been developing. Under Continue, identify something today has confirmed you already do well. Under Reconsider, name an approach you would now look at differently.
For each, ask how you will know it worked, and what evidence will help you judge that. For your Start, these sentence stems may help: I will. The barrier or demand I am addressing is. I will notice. I need to consult. I will review this on.
Choose something within your influence. Keep the focus on the student's access, participation and experience. An action that depends on the student appearing less Autistic needs rethinking. Consider what you can change in the environment, task or response, and whose knowledge you need before or while you try it.
Allow eight minutes. This is your card. When you're ready, share one thing with someone at your table if you feel comfortable. Pause the video now, and we'll return to check the action.

### Slide 4

Let's look once more at your Start. Is it small enough to begin? Does it name a barrier or demand you can act on? Have you identified when you'll consult the student, family or team, and when you'll review what happens?
Check the evidence you plan to notice. If the measure is simply that the student becomes quieter or complies more readily, look again. What will help you understand their access, participation and experience? Include their perspective in a way that works for them.
Keep the action small, testable and open to revision. Your Continue and Reconsider are useful alongside it: one recognises a practice worth sustaining, and the other keeps your learning open.

### Slide 5

Now let's plan the next conversation, on page twenty-eight. Bring forward your Session Five bridge notes from page twenty-five, and the process change you chose in the power-dynamics reflection on page twenty-three.
Choose one real conversation you can have within two weeks, with a student’s family, the student themselves, or a colleague who teaches them. Where you can, choose the family or student conversation so their knowledge can shape the work directly. A colleague handover is also a real choice in secondary: keeping support consistent across teachers is part of partnership. Begin by writing the purpose in one sentence. What do you need to understand? Leave room for their perspective to shape the conversation.
Plan what you'll share, beginning with a strength, what you'll ask specifically, and what you'll offer. Include how the student's communication and preferences will be heard. Write two open questions, and note what you will listen for.
If you’re planning a handover, return to what should travel between people and places on page eleven. How will the student’s own voice travel too? A conversation about them still needs their perspective in it.
Now write your opening sentence, word for word. You can draw on the opening question from The first two minutes on page twenty-four. Say it quietly to yourself if that helps you hear how it might land. Is it clear and respectful? Does it invite something you don't already know?
Name one possible shared next step, leaving room for the conversation to change it. Record how you'll follow up, and when, where and how the conversation will happen. Apply your process change to those arrangements. Use initials or roles in your workshop notes.
You have eight minutes for the full activity. Pause here to make your plan, then resume.

Take a final look at your conversation plan. Have you prepared to listen, as well as to speak? Does the plan leave something meaningful to decide together? Have you made a way for the student's own perspective to be included?
You can prepare your opening and your questions without scripting the other person's response. If new knowledge changes the next step you had in mind, the conversation is doing useful work.
Keep the time and follow-up visible in your plan. One design action and one conversation now connect what you've learned today to the people and setting where you will use it.
