### Slide 4

Many of us learnt five senses: touch, vision, taste, hearing and smell. This workshop uses eight. The other three are proprioception, vestibular sense and interoception.

Touch includes information about texture, pressure and temperature. Vision gives information about things such as colour, shape, depth and position. Gustatory and oral experience includes taste, texture, temperature and the sensation of chewing. Hearing includes pitch, loudness, distance and familiar sounds. Smell can also be closely connected with memory.

Proprioception gives us information about where our body is in space, drawing on signals from our muscles and joints. Pressure or movement may be something a student seeks for that input. The vestibular sense relates to balance and movement, including the speed and direction of head movement. Rocking or spinning may serve a regulating purpose for some students.

Interoception relates to signals from inside the body. We'll return to it later in the session.

These senses work together. A single classroom experience can involve several at once. The three less familiar senses can be especially useful to consider when something about a student's experience isn't yet clear to us.

Keep these as possibilities to explore, rather than explanations we can assign by watching alone. Students will differ. There is also an interoception webinar in the Positive Partnerships resources if you want to follow up.

### Slide 5

Here is the sequence again: sensory information, processing, response. Imagine you're wearing a jumper and becoming hot. Information about temperature arrives. You make sense of that information, including what it means in relation to your experience, and you might respond by taking the jumper off.

In a classroom, this processing helps us keep track of our bodies and surroundings, notice what matters and direct attention. It is part of safety, learning and engagement. There is a great deal happening before a response becomes visible to someone else.

That is why we need to look beyond the behaviour we can see. Sensory processing is not simply a deliberate choice a student makes about how the world affects them. When we notice a response, we can ask what information is arriving, what the environment is asking of the student, and what we need to understand with them.

### Slide 9

This slide introduces four sensory response patterns from occupational therapist Winnie Dunn's framework. You may encounter this language in an occupational therapy report or a Sensory Profile School Companion questionnaire. The patterns are useful lenses, rather than fixed types of person.

Registration describes a pattern of noticing less sensory information or missing cues that others notice. Seeking describes actively looking for more input, perhaps through movement, touch or sound.

Sensitivity describes noticing sensory input readily and being affected by it, without necessarily taking action to reduce it. Avoiding describes actively trying to reduce input, perhaps by moving away from a crowd or covering the ears.

The slide also uses under-responsive and over-responsive language, and distinguishes more passive responses from active ways of changing the input. Keep that distinction connected to the student's experience, rather than treating the labels as a judgement.

A student may show different patterns for different senses. Their responses may also change with the context, the load and the day. We are looking at nervous-system responses, not simply a decision to cooperate or avoid a task.

So use the framework to sharpen your questions. What input is present? What has the student communicated? What pattern might be worth exploring? An observation gives us a starting point, not certainty about what is happening inside.

### Slide 12

A detail-first approach offers one way of understanding Autistic thinking. Top-down processing uses expectations and the wider picture to help filter information. Bottom-up processing begins with the details and builds towards the whole. A detail-first account places more emphasis on that detailed information arriving and being worked through.

That can help us consider both depth and load. Noticing a change, an inconsistency or a pattern can be a strength. Processing a great deal of detail can also make a situation demanding. Neither conclusion tells us exactly what a particular student is experiencing.

The strengths on this slide include pattern recognition, systems thinking, deep memory, logical precision, honesty and directness, depth of connection, and intense engagement. Take each seriously as something valuable in itself. Strengths are part of a student's present identity and learning, rather than compensation for a difficulty or only future potential.

You might recognise connections with sensory intensity, literal language or deep focus. Keep these as connections to explore, rather than assuming a thinking process from behaviour alone.

Turn to Strengths I can see on page eight of your Participant Guide. For the students you have in mind, note the strengths you recognise. What evidence supports that understanding? What might you be missing if your attention is mainly drawn to difficulty?

### Slide 13

Think for a moment about your own attention. Does it move across many topics, or settle deeply into one? That question introduces monotropism: a framework for understanding attention focused deeply on fewer things at a time.

Dinah Murray, Mike Lesser and Wenn Lawson developed the framework, and Autistic community voices have continued to extend it. One image is a torch beam and a floodlight. A floodlight spreads across a wide area; a torch illuminates a narrower area intensely. Another is a river with many channels compared with one deep channel. These are ways to picture different patterns of attention, rather than a test for sorting people.

Deep interests can provide a strong route into learning, memory and joy. Flow, that full absorption in an activity, is something worth protecting. It is more useful to recognise that engagement than dismiss it as an obsession.

Now picture a student absorbed in a construction task, drawing or researching space. Asking them to stop when the bell goes may require a substantial shift. Tendril theory offers another image: attention reaching into an activity, then needing time to withdraw before moving elsewhere.

Transitions can have a real cost. Warning and scaffolding may help make that shift more manageable. Keep asking what support fits this student and this moment. Detail-first processing helps us consider how information arrives; deep focus helps us consider where attention goes.

### Slide 14

Executive functioning gives us another set of questions about the demands of the school day. Let's name the ten areas on the slide.

Planning is working out what needs to happen. Organisation is arranging materials and information. Time management involves judging and using time. Working memory helps hold information in mind while we use it. Metacognition means thinking about our own thinking.

Emotional regulation concerns managing emotional responses. Flexibility involves adapting when something changes. Attention is directing and sustaining focus. Task initiation is getting started. Persistence is continuing with a task.

Now consider an ordinary classroom activity. What does it ask a student to plan, start, remember, sequence, organise, switch and complete? A seemingly simple instruction can contain several demands. Interruptions, timing and the way instructions are presented can change what is required.

These terms are lenses for inquiry, not a checklist to apply to a student. A delay in starting does not tell us, by itself, that someone lacks effort or motivation. Useful support responds to the specific demand in the specific context.

As you look across the ten areas, consider where each appears in the school day for your students, and where task design, timing or support might make access more workable.

### Slide 20

Interoception is about understanding our body's internal signals. It helps us answer the question, how am I doing? Am I hot or cold? Am I hungry or thirsty? Do I need the bathroom? Am I tired? These signals also contribute to noticing changes as we become upset and to regulation.

The other term on this slide is alexithymia: difficulty identifying and describing emotions and feelings. Keep that definition clear. Identifying what is happening and finding words to describe it can be difficult.

The questions on screen invite us to notice how much interpretation can be involved. Am I excited or am I anxious? Is crying always sadness? What is my friend feeling?

We shouldn't assume the answer from outward behaviour. Crying alone, for example, does not establish one particular feeling. And an apparently calm surface doesn't tell us everything about what is happening inside.

These terms help us recognise why understanding internal experience matters. They are a reason to stay open to the student's communication and to build understanding together, rather than to decide their internal state for them.

### Slide 21

Sometimes a student experiences a build-up inside, while the surface gives us little indication. If we wait until distress is obvious, we may miss earlier opportunities to respond.

The more we understand a student's early signs, the better placed we are to recognise when support might be useful. That understanding needs to be built with each student, their family and other staff. No one person's observation gives us the whole picture.

Think of a shared map: what the student communicates, what different people notice, what remains uncertain, and how people can respond consistently. The map needs to remain open to revision as circumstances and capacity change.

An analogy or an observation can help start a conversation. It doesn't replace the student's own communication. Keep asking how the student can contribute, including in ways other than a spoken explanation, and what the environment can do with the information they share.
