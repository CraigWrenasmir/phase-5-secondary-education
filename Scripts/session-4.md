### Slide 8

Consider this example: a student who knows the subject misses an assessment submission. Before assuming that they didn’t care, what else would you want to understand? Think about starting, planning, holding steps in mind and managing time.
The four moves on this slide make those demands clearer. Publish assessment schedules early. Break tasks into smaller parts with visible milestones. Show an exemplar of the finished thing so students can see what they are working towards. Make submission steps literal: where does the work go, in what format, and by when?
If a student needs help holding information in mind, put useful information where they can return to it. Written first steps, instructions that stay visible and a clear clock or timer are examples. Match them to the demand and check with students whether they help.
Think about homework too. How much of the task depends on organisation and time, as well as subject knowledge? An unfinished task may leave questions about access that a mark alone won’t answer.
These supports help students organise themselves to show what they know. Keep the learning purpose clear and involve the relevant school staff where assessment policy or formal adjustments sit beyond one teacher’s decision. Return to your brief: which demand could you make more visible or manageable?

### Slide 9

Predictability means having accessible information about routine and change. It doesn’t mean every day must be identical, or that we can promise nothing unexpected will happen.
Think about how much information in a secondary day is spoken once, implied, or held by adults. It may change between periods. If I couldn’t rely on remembering what someone told me, how would I know what was happening across today’s timetable?
A useful support helps students answer four questions: what is happening, what is changing, what comes next, and what can I do if the plan changes? Give information early enough to be useful, in every class and on relief days.
Imagine a Year Nine student arrives at period three to find a relief teacher, another room and a changed task. Could they find out before arriving? Is the change available somewhere they can check, such as the class page or timetable app? Does the relief plan keep the usual starting routine? Can the student ask for information or support in a way that works for them?
Return to the day audit. When something changes, how do students know? Consistent routines, warning before transitions and information in more than one form can help. Review the support through actual experience and feedback.

### Slide 11

With any visual support, start by asking what the student needs to find, understand, remember or communicate. Then choose a form that can make that information clear. It might be an object, photograph, symbol, written word, checklist, map or choice support.
In secondary, that might be instructions that stay on the board or class page, a task map, a checklist or an assessment calendar. Choose a format that is respectful and suits the young person’s age and preferences. A support available to the whole class may feel more comfortable than one that singles a student out.
Visual does not automatically mean accessible. The student needs to understand what it represents and be able to use it at the point of need. Keep unnecessary detail out, explain how it works, and check its meaning with the student.
Page nineteen gives four quick-review questions. Can the student understand it? Can they access it when they need it? Does it reflect what is actually happening, including change? Does it give useful information or a way to respond?
Keep understanding and expressing in view. A visual support may help someone understand, while A A C provides ways to communicate. Access to A A C doesn’t mean every spoken instruction or schedule is understood. Keep the student’s communication system available in every class, during transitions and changes, and recognise responses beyond speech.
The materials refer to a Visual Supports Checklist and an online webinar for further learning, but their access details still need to be confirmed. The review questions on page nineteen are ready to use. A checklist supports personalisation and consultation; it does not prescribe one format for every student. Keep asking: does this give this student useful information they can understand, access and act on?

### Slide 17

Support is a continuum, rather than one intervention that answers every need. Start with an inclusive environment: predictable routines across classes, clear expectations, assessment schedules published early, and a school culture where different ways of participating are accepted and valued.
Then consider universal supports available to many students. Instructions can be offered in spoken, written and visual forms. Students may have flexible ways to demonstrate learning, with exemplars and a class page where task information stays available.
Targeted support responds to a more specific need. For example, a small group might benefit from key vocabulary being introduced before a lesson, or from check-ins on assessment milestones.
Individual adjustments respond to the particular student’s access and participation needs. Examples might include a quieter workspace for an assessment, additional processing time, a personalised task breakdown or another way to demonstrate learning. What is appropriate depends on the student and the context, informed by consultation and evidence.
Look back at the design check. What is available to everyone? What might need to be targeted? What needs individual adjustment? Academic adjustments sit within this wider picture. A diagnosis alone doesn’t tell us which adjustment will help. Connect the response with your observations, the day map, and student and family knowledge, then review it through use and feedback.

### Slide 18

Reasonable adjustments are changes or supports that help students access and participate in education in a way that works for them. They should be individualised, build on strengths, remove barriers, and be practical and effective. Informed decisions and review help us find out whether they are enabling the student.
The diagram places adjustments within the wider work of environmental design and Universal Design for Learning. It also names the four N C C D levels of adjustment: quality differentiated teaching practice, supplementary, substantial and extensive.
Keep two things distinct. Reasonable adjustments are the changes and supports provided. The N C C D is the process for collecting information about adjustments for students with disability. Evidence of what has been provided and monitored matters; a diagnostic label by itself doesn’t choose an adjustment or tell the whole story.
Within curriculum learning, we want support to build capability, confidence, self-advocacy and independence. The question is whether students can access and participate, with support that empowers them. For formal requirements and decisions, use current official guidance and your school or system processes.
